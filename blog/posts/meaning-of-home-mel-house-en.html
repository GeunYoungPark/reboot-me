As House Prices Rose, What Did a Home Come to Mean to Us?

Reflections on Mel’s old house

When we first moved in, an elderly man named Mel lived next door.

At the time, he must have been around eighty. He was already old, but he was still living in his own home. It was not a new house. It was not a glamorous house. It was an old house. But within its walls, the life of one family had quietly gathered over many years.

Mel told us that he had built the house himself about fifty years earlier. When he first moved in, he planted a jacaranda tree in the backyard. Over the years, that tree grew enormous. Its branches stretched over the fence and reached into our yard. Every spring, its purple flowers bloomed brilliantly, filling the whole area with vivid colour. I still remember an old photograph of his daughter standing beneath that tree.

Mel lived in that house with his wife. His wife could no longer use her legs properly, and she spent most of her days sitting down. Mel cared for her for as long as he possibly could. But as time passed, his own knees became badly worn and painful. Eventually, he had no choice but to move his wife into aged care.

Not long after that, she had a fall in the aged care home and injured her hip. Within two weeks, she passed away.

Everything happened so quickly. I still remember how deeply Mel grieved. We cried with him.

Even after his wife died, Mel stayed in the house.

For a while, he lived there alone. Then, sometime later, his son moved back home. His son did not have a family of his own. He, too, was getting older, and his health was not very good. But he had somewhere to return to. He had his father. He had the family home.

Mel once told us that he was glad his son was living with him.

That old house became a place where a father and son could spend the later stage of life together. There was no modern kitchen. No beautifully renovated space. No stylish living room. But the house was comfortable. It was familiar. And it was theirs.

In the evenings, I would sometimes see the two old men preparing dinner together through the kitchen window. Looking at their silhouettes, I would quietly think, “They are still here. They are still managing together in this house.”

One day, Mel showed us some old family photographs. His wife had been a Dalmatian breeder, and they had raised seven Dalmatians in that house. In the photos, she was young and beautiful, and the home looked full of life. You could feel that, for a long time, it had been a happy place.

About two years ago, when Mel was probably around ninety-two, he finally sold the house. His son must have been around seventy. By then, both father and son were in poor health, and it had become too difficult for them to continue living independently. In the end, they both had to move into aged care.

The house had not been renovated at all. There was no special open home and no auction. It seemed to be sold more for the value of the land than for the house itself. But fortunately, it sold for a good price. That money allowed both of them to spend the rest of their lives with proper care.

That was when I truly understood the importance of a home.

When Mel was young, the house was where his family lived. When his wife became frail, it was where the two of them could still lean on each other. After she passed away, it became the familiar place that allowed Mel to keep going. When his son returned, it became a place that received him, too. And at the very end, it became an asset that helped them move into the next stage of life.

It was not simply property ownership.

It was protection.

Of course, not everyone can buy a home without debt. Many people spend decades paying off a mortgage in order to own one. But watching Mel’s house made me think about something.

For a home to truly help us, it should not become a burden that grows heavier with time. It should become easier to live in — financially, physically, and emotionally.

A house that can only be maintained by working endlessly to make large monthly repayments may not give a person real rest. This becomes even more true as we get older and our bodies become weaker. At that stage, the home we need may not be bigger or more impressive.

It may simply be a house where we can spend the whole day comfortably. A house that does not cost too much to maintain. A house where we can continue living even when our bodies become frail. A house with good neighbours and old friends nearby. And finally, a house that gives us enough room — financially and emotionally — to move into the next stage when the time comes.

Mel’s house was that kind of house.

It was not glamorous, but it was enough. It was not new, but it endured. And in the end, it helped him move forward into the next chapter of life.

To me, that is what a good home is.

Mel’s house protected his life.
