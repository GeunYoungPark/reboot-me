The Party Is Over, and the Bill Has Arrived

A personal reflection on defective apartments, falling prices, and the changing mood of the Australian property market.

Lately, I have been feeling something strange when I look at the Australian property market.

I recently heard a story about a one-bedroom apartment near North Ryde Metro. A young couple wanted to buy it because they were tired of renting, and the apartment was almost $100,000 cheaper than similar apartments in the area. The problem was that the building had defects. The owner really wanted to sell it, so the initial deposit was only $1,650, and the cooling-off period was extended several times to give the buyers more time.

But in the end, the sale still could not go through. The mortgage was rejected three times because the bank did not want to lend against that apartment. Even though the buyers were willing to take the risk, the bank was not. That story stayed with me, because defective apartments are becoming a serious issue in Australia. It made me realise that some properties are not just hard to sell because of price. They are hard to sell because banks, insurers, and future buyers may not want to touch them.

Then I started hearing more stories from Melbourne. Thousands of newly completed apartments are reportedly sitting unsold, even in major locations. That sounds strange when we keep hearing that Australia has a housing shortage. But the issue seems to be price. New apartments are too expensive to build and too expensive to buy. If the supply cost is around $12,500 per square metre, but buyers believe similar apartments may be worth closer to $8,000 per square metre, who would want to pay the higher price? Especially when Melbourne apartment values have already been falling, and many people expect them to fall further.

And then there is the recent story about first home buyers who bought with only a 5% deposit. Some of them bought near the peak, especially late last year. Now, as prices fall, some are already facing the risk of negative equity. That means the value of their home may be lower than the mortgage they still owe.

That feels sad to me. Many of these buyers are probably young people or young couples who simply wanted to get into the market. The government said they could buy with a 5% deposit and avoid LMI through a guarantee. But if prices fall soon after they buy, they are left with a very heavy loan and very little protection.

These stories are different, but they seem to point in the same direction.

For years, many people believed house prices would keep rising. Homes were not just homes anymore. They became wealth, security, refinancing power, and a reason to borrow more.

But now I feel the mood is changing.

The party is over.
And the bill has arrived.
