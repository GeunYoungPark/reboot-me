The Hidden Cost of Working Through Pain

After 50, We Must Take the Body’s Recovery Capacity Seriously

After 50, we begin to experience one important change.
The body no longer comes back as easily as before.

Fatigue that used to disappear after a day or two now lingers for several days.
Even a small amount of overwork can leave the joints stiff and the muscles sore for longer.
Even after sleeping, the body does not always feel recharged.

This change is not just a feeling.

A recent Stanford Medicine study reported that human aging does not progress at one steady speed, but may show major biological shifts in the mid-40s and early 60s. Researchers analysed thousands of molecules and microbes and found that around 81% of the molecules studied showed non-linear changes, meaning they changed more significantly at certain ages than at other times. The largest shifts appeared in two periods: the mid-40s and the early 60s. [1]

“Around 81% of the molecules studied showed non-linear fluctuations, with the largest changes occurring in the mid-40s and early 60s.”
— Stanford Medicine News Center, 2024

So when we feel, at some point, that we have “suddenly aged,” it may not be entirely our imagination.

The body changes quietly, but clearly.
Metabolism changes, immune responses change, and the recovery speed of muscles and joints changes.

That is why, after 50, we must take recovery capacity seriously.

Recovery is not just a nice word for “health.”
Recovery is the strength that allows us to keep working.
It is the strength that allows us to earn money, care for family, and maintain our life.

I think of it as a strategic asset.

Our body cannot be easily replaced.
Just as lost time cannot be bought back, a body that has been seriously damaged cannot simply be exchanged for a new one.
A car can be replaced, a phone can be replaced, and even a house can be changed.
But the body is not so easily replaced.

So the body is not just a tool.
Like time, it is one of our most fundamental assets.

And yet, strangely, whenever we face an important choice, we often push the body to the very end of our priorities.

It is not that we do not know our body is tired.
We know we need to rest.
We have heard many times that health is important.

But real life does not move that way.

There is work that must be done today.
There is money that must be earned today.
There is an appointment we cannot miss.
At work, we may be in a responsible position, and when we come home, another set of responsibilities is waiting.
We may need to care about our parents’ health, worry about our children, or keep managing the many small and large tasks of family life.
The cost of living is high, there are many expenses, and after 50, the fear of redundancy or retirement comes closer.

So people choose work, even when their body is struggling.

As a massage therapist, I often meet people in this situation.
Many of my female clients over 50 are truly remarkable.

At work, they hold responsible positions.
At home, they cannot completely step away from family and daily life.
Even when their body is exhausted, they continue to finish what they believe they must do.

It feels as if they have lived all these years with a powerful engine that has never been allowed to stop.

But at some point, that engine no longer recovers the way it used to.

“I worked more than 10 hours every day last week.”
“I was so exhausted yesterday that I just had to come in.”
“I know I should rest, but I don’t have time.”

I hear these kinds of comments often.

When I hear them, I feel conflicted.
Because I know they are not pushing themselves because they want to suffer.
Underneath it all, there is responsibility, financial pressure, and the anxiety that they must not stop.

But at the same time, I feel worried.

They endure with an aching body, take painkillers, and finish the day’s work.
Then the next day, they come for a massage to loosen the body and go back to work again.
Sometimes massage becomes less like true rest and more like temporary repair before another round of overwork.

At those moments, I find myself asking:

Is the money earned with an aching body really a profit?

When we earn money, we do not usually look only at the income.
We consider transport costs, food costs, and tax.
If we run a business, we consider materials, rent, labour, and equipment costs.

But strangely, one of the most important costs is often left out.

The recovery needed for the body to return after doing the work.

Of course, not all work is bad.
Working with a body that is not in pain is fine.
If we use 60 or 70% of our energy and recover after a night’s sleep, that work is sustainable.

That kind of work allows us to use the body, earn money, and live again tomorrow.

But money earned with a body that is already tired,
with a body that is already aching,
with the help of painkillers,
needs to be seen differently.

How many days will recovery take after earning that money?
Will the pain become worse and force us to reduce work next week?
Will we need to take more medication?
Will our confidence in our body decrease?
Will our face look exhausted, our energy drop, and our strength to work again disappear?

When we include these things, it becomes difficult to say that the money earned today is pure profit.

It becomes clearer if we think about a car.

Imagine we need to use a car to go to a job.
But the road is so rough that after doing that job once, the car is badly damaged.
We earned money today, but the next day, the car must be repaired.
In a serious case, we may not be able to use that car for several days.

Could we easily call that a good income?

The body is the same.

The body is not the opposite of money.
The body is the most basic asset that allows us to earn money, work, meet people, care for family, and continue our life.

But we take the body too much for granted.

“I’ll be fine after sleeping for a day or two.”
“I can just take some medicine and keep going.”
“If I just get through this, I can rest later.”

When we were younger, this was possible to some extent.
Even after several days of overwork, a weekend rest could make us feel better.
Even after intense exercise, the body often came back after a few days.
Sometimes it even felt stronger, as if new muscle had grown.

But after 50, that formula gradually stops working.

Some parts of the body become stronger when we use them.
But without recovery, some parts simply wear down.
Especially when we keep using a body with reduced recovery capacity as if it were still young, there comes a point when recovery can no longer keep up with the workload.

From that point, the body is not simply tired.
Its ability to come back becomes weaker.

That is why, after 50, the question must change.

We should not only ask whether we can do the work.
We should ask whether we can recover after doing it.

We should not only ask whether we can earn money today.
We should ask whether we will still have a body that can work tomorrow.

Eating healthy food, exercising, and receiving massage are all important.
But there is something we need to look at before that.

How am I working?
How am I using my body?
Is there balance between the energy I spend and the energy I recover?

A good choice after 50 is not a choice that avoids using the body altogether.
It is not about protecting the body so much that we do nothing.

A good choice is a sustainable choice.

It is a choice that does not burn out the body and mind completely, but leaves some room.
It is a choice that allows us to work today and begin again tomorrow.

I think one ideal standard for work is this:

Using about 70% of my energy,
and leaving about 30% as room for recovery.

Of course, reality is not always that ideal.
Life in our 50s is not quiet or easy.
The cost of living is high, there are many expenses, there are many things to worry about, and the body is no longer the same.
Responsibilities at home and at work do not easily decrease.

That is why it is harder.

But because it is hard, we need to think about it more carefully.

We should not only look at whether the work brings money.
We should also look at whether the body can return after doing that work.

Money earned with an aching body is not always bad.
Sometimes we have no choice but to do what must be done.
We all live inside that reality.

But it is dangerous to keep repeating it while thinking, “It will be fine.”

At some point, the body may not come back the way it used to.
The baseline of energy may drop, recovery may slow down, and confidence in the body may disappear.

At that point, we may lose something greater than the money we earned today.

So after 50, we should not look only at today’s income.
We must also look at the body that allows us to work tomorrow.

The body is a strategic asset.
Like time, it cannot be turned back and cannot be easily replaced.

We need to ask ourselves at least once:
Am I wearing down this asset little by little every day?
Or am I using it in a way that allows it to recover?

The choice we truly need after 50 is not to sacrifice the body for money.
Nor is it to give up money and protect only the body.

**It is the choice to see the recovery capacity of the body as one of the most important conditions
for continuing to live, work, and move forward.**
